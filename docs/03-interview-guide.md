# SafeArrival interview guide

## Purpose

Desk research suggests that vehicle hijacking can happen at home or on residential streets and that safety/check-in services already exist. It does not prove that the SafeArrival idea solves a real unmet need. These interviews should discover people's actual routines before pitching a solution.

The Samsung assignment's own-idea route requires conversations with at least three people who have the problem. Interview at least three people who regularly arrive home late, are responsible for checking on someone returning late, or have relevant first-hand experience. Do not pressure anyone to recount a traumatic event.

## Before the conversation

- Ask if they are willing to have a short conversation for an internship project.
- Explain that participation is optional and they can skip questions or stop.
- Do not record audio/video unless they explicitly agree.
- Do not request names of alleged offenders, exact addresses, phone numbers, vehicle registrations, case numbers, or other people's personal details.
- Use anonymous labels such as Participant A.
- Do not promise the app will prevent crime or dispatch emergency responders.

## Interview sequence

### A. Warm-up and context

1. What does a normal trip home look like for you on a day you arrive later than you would like?
2. How do you usually let someone know you have left or arrived?
3. Who, if anyone, expects to hear from you?

### B. Current behavior and pain

4. Tell me about the last time arriving home felt difficult, uncertain, or stressful. What happened, step by step?
5. At what point, if any, did you want help or want someone to know where you were?
6. What did you do? What happened next?
7. What was frustrating, unreliable, costly, or hard to remember about that process?
8. How do you handle the gate or the last few minutes before getting inside?
9. Where is your phone during that part? Can you access it? What if you choose not to?
10. Have you used a tracker, panic button, safety app, live location, family group, security company, or neighbourhood watch? What worked or failed?

### C. Consequences and frequency

11. How often does this situation or concern come up for you?
12. What happens if nobody knows you are delayed or cannot reach you?
13. Who would be able to act on a check-in alert, and what would they actually do?
14. What would make a false alarm disruptive or unsafe?

Avoid asking “Would you use this app?” before learning actual behavior. People may be polite or hypothetical. Ask for examples and past actions.

### D. Concept reaction (only after current behavior)

Describe the concept neutrally: “Imagine a service where you start a trip, choose a time window for arriving, and a trusted person is notified if you do not confirm you are safe. What is your reaction?”

15. What part, if any, would help you?
16. What part would you remove or change?
17. What would make you trust it? What would make you avoid it?
18. Would the midway prompt be useful or irritating? When would it be safe to receive it?
19. How much time do you need between reaching home and confirming you are safely inside? How would you want to set that?
20. If you did not confirm, who should be contacted and what should the message say?
21. Should the system call you, message you, or leave the decision to the trusted contact? Why?
22. What should happen if the phone is off, left in the car, out of data, or has inaccurate location?
23. Would a hosted web app be acceptable, or would you expect an installed app/device? What makes you say that?
24. What existing tool would you use instead?

### E. Close

25. What important question did I not ask?
26. Is there anyone else with this routine who might be willing to speak with me?

Thank them. Do not ask for their phone number in the research document. If follow-up is needed, obtain a separate opt-in and store contact details outside the repository.

## Interview quality reminders

- Ask about a real recent example, not only opinions about a hypothetical app.
- Ask open questions; do not tell people what they should be afraid of.
- Separate observation (“phone stays in car”) from interpretation (“therefore a wearable is required”).
- Record negative evidence and disagreement.
- Do not treat three interviews as representative of South Africa. They provide early qualitative evidence for a student project.
- Use fictional labels and remove details that could identify a person or incident.
